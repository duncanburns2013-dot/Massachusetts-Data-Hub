What you're billed is the first layer, not the last.

Visible taxes — the ones with your name on the notice — are the only part most burden estimates count. Four more layers sit underneath: the employer half of payroll tax, tax embedded in prices and wages, policy costs collected through utility bills, and this year's borrowing, which is a tax with the due date left blank.

Every figure comes from a published rate applied to a number you can verify on your own return, deed, or receipt. No incidence assumption required.

2 · Paid on your behalf

$0
Solid fillStatutory · incidence near-consensus

The employer half of FICA. CBO, JCT and Treasury all model this as borne by the worker through lower wages. The least controversial hidden layer.

3 · Embedded in prices and wages

$0
HatchedCollections audited · incidence modeled

Corporate tax is collected from firms and paid by people — the question is which people. JCT and CBO split it roughly 75% to capital owners, 25% to labor. Because capital ownership is concentrated, a wage-earning household bears far less than the per-household average. Move the sliders to see how much of the answer is the assumption.

4 · Collected through the meter

$0
HatchedTariff-verifiable · not labeled tax

Legislatively mandated program costs recovered through regulated rates. Functionally a consumption tax with an unelected rate-setter. The inclusion rule is stated rather than assumed: a charge counts here if it exists because of a legislative or regulatory mandate, not because of the physical cost of generating and delivering energy. On that test the electric riders are energy efficiency, net metering recovery, SMART solar, the EV programme, renewable energy and sector modernisation — 3.789¢/kWh on the current Eversource tariff. Distribution, transmission, revenue decoupling and stranded cost are excluded as infrastructure or mechanism.

Only partly verifiable, despite the claim usually made for it. The tariff's own footnote admits the Distribution charge already contains the Long Term Renewable Contract Adjustment — that is 83C/83D offshore wind — plus solar programme and grid modernisation costs, none broken out. RPS/CES compliance sits inside supply, also not broken out. So the figure above is a floor. Gas is the bigger surprise: energy efficiency at 41.70¢/therm plus GSEP at 17.38¢ comes to 59.08¢ — more than the 38.42¢/therm the gas itself costs.

5 · Deferred to later

$0
DottedTotals audited · allocation is a choice

Government consumed this much more than it collected. It will be settled by future taxation, inflation, or reduced future services — the only open question is by whom. The allocation basis is not a fact, it's a position, which is why it's a dropdown and not a constant.

State and local pension debt is deliberately excluded from this total. The test for every line on this page is whether a household actually pays it. An unfunded pension liability isn't billed to anyone — it's a claim on future tax capacity, and the portion being collected today arrives through an appropriation funded by the income and property taxes Layer 1 already counts. Counting it here would count it twice. The obligation is real, so it is disclosed rather than dropped, and told properly on the pension dashboard.

Reconciliation check

Where the layers land across the income distribution

Same assumptions, applied at eight income levels. The visible layer is progressive; every hidden layer flattens or reverses that, because property tax, utility charges and per-household deficit allocation don't scale with earnings. Property tax, utility volumes and investment holdings are held at your entered values across all eight levels — that isolates the rate structure, but it is not how a real household at $50k would live.

Method note. Layers 1 and 2 are arithmetic. Layers 3 and 5 are audited totals divided by a contested denominator — the model exposes the denominator rather than burying it. Layer 4 is the only one where better sourcing materially moves the number, and it is also the one you can source hardest, since every component is a filed tariff. Sales tax is modeled as 6.25% of a taxable-consumption share of income that declines with income, approximating CEX behaviour; replace with BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey decile shares for publication.

On investment income. Massachusetts taxes interest, dividends and long-term capital gains at 5%, short-term gains at 8.5%, and counts all of it toward the 4% surtax threshold — which is why a single large realisation can trigger the surtax in a year when wages alone would not. Tax-deferred balances are not taxed while they grow; only withdrawals are, as ordinary income. Set a withdrawal figure to model a retiree rather than an accumulator.

Not counted: tariffs and customs duties embedded in goods, federal excise beyond motor fuel, municipal debt service already inside the visible bill, the 0.75% local meals option, estate tax, deed excise, and the inflation tax. Including them raises the total; leaving them out keeps the figure conservative.

On exactness, and what this page is claiming. Every figure here is a point-in-time reading of a filed number, not a standing fact. Statutory rates move when the legislature moves them. Fiscal-year revenue and Census figures re-close on a cycle. Utility tariffs are refiled mid-year, and pension liabilities move with asset returns — so those two are snapshots in the strictest sense, and the stamp on the card above says which vintage you are looking at. What a date cannot fix is a figure that never had a source: those are marked in the Status column of the ledger and flagged on the card, because calling a placeholder "current as of" would disguise the actual problem. Re-run update-burden-constants.py against the constants file to refresh, and the build will refuse to publish while unsourced defaults remain.
