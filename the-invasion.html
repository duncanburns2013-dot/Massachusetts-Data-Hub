The Shift: Emergency Shelter → HomeBASE

The FY27 budget signed on July 9, 2026 keeps the total cost in place but changes the label. As the state closed its emergency shelter hotels (all shut by August 2025), it moved families onto HomeBASE — a rental-assistance program whose cost has quietly exploded.

Same Families, New Line Item

HomeBASE pays up to $30,000+ per family in rent and moving costs to transition households out of shelter. Enrollment jumped from ~1,500 families in early 2023 to over 7,700 by 2025, and annual spending rose 718% — from $12M to $101M — even as the emergency-shelter budget was cut. The crisis didn't end; it changed accounts.

The Paper Trail Goes Cold

The emergency shelter system was countable. Families were in known buildings, the caseload was published every two weeks, and the spending was reported in near-real time. HomeBASE moved those families into private rentals scattered across the state — and the reporting stopped at almost exactly the same moment.

The population didn't shrink. It dispersed.

Emergency shelter caseload fell from its peak to roughly 4,800 families, and the state is shrinking the system toward ~3,200 units. But 7,700+ families are on HomeBASE, which pays up to $30,000 over two years toward private-market rent. Those households are still being housed at public expense — they are simply no longer housed anywhere the public can count them.

What we could not verify: the November 2025 closeout supplemental budget contained language requiring public biweekly spending reports and regular HomeBASE reporting — including household size, age, gender and immigration status. We could not confirm from public sources whether that provision was enacted, or whether any such HomeBASE report has since been published. If you can point to one, open an issue and this page will be corrected.

The questions this raises

None of this establishes wrongdoing, and the reporting lapse has a mundane explanation available: the statute requiring real-time disclosure was written to expire with fiscal 2025, and it did. But the effect is the same either way — the most expensive housing program the state runs is now also the least visible one. How many HomeBASE households are there today, in which municipalities, at what average cost, for how long, and how many are newly arrived? Every one of those was answerable for emergency shelter. None is answerable for HomeBASE from public records.

This table mixes two different methods

Every row except Massachusetts comes from FAIR's 2023 national study, which estimates the total state-and-local cost of illegal immigration across education, healthcare, justice and welfare. The Massachusetts row is this project's own FY25 estimate, built from shelter, education, services and healthcare for the emergency-assistance population. Different methodologies, different years, and different populations — so read the table for order of magnitude only, not as a like-for-like ranking.

Correction, August 2026. The per-capita column previously showed $537 for Massachusetts. The Cost Shift

The Congressional Budget Office found the immigration surge reduced federal deficits by $0.9 trillion over 10 years—because immigrants pay payroll taxes for Social Security they may never collect. But state and local governments bore a net cost of $9.8 billion in 2023 alone for education, shelter, and healthcare. The feds profit while states bleed.

The Bigger Number: Registered Lobbying

Employee campaign donations are the small line. ACLU of Massachusetts is a registered lobbying client with the Secretary of the Commonwealth, and its disclosed lobbying spend is roughly 40× the donation total above.

$2.33 Million, 2020–2025

Six complete registration years of in-house lobbyist salaries, payments to the outside firm Isaacson Political Consulting, and disclosed operating expenses. 2025 was the peak year at $465,709. The first half of 2026 already adds another $208,217.

Important scope caveat: ACLU-MA's registered purpose is "all matters related to the protection and advancement of civil liberties and civil rights." That is broad and not immigration-specific. This figure should not be described as immigration lobbying — unlike MIRA's, whose registration names immigration bills directly.
