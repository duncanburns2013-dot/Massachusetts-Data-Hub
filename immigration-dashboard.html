The Shift: Emergency Shelter → HomeBASE

The FY27 budget signed on July 9, 2026 keeps the total cost in place but changes the label. As the state closed its emergency shelter hotels (all shut by August 2025), it moved families onto HomeBASE — a rental-assistance program whose cost has quietly exploded.

Same Families, New Line Item

HomeBASE pays up to $30,000+ per family in rent and moving costs to transition households out of shelter. Enrollment jumped from ~1,500 families in early 2023 to over 7,700 by 2025, and annual spending rose 718% — from $12M to $101M — even as the emergency-shelter budget was cut. The crisis didn't end; it changed accounts.

The Paper Trail Goes Cold

The emergency shelter system was countable. Families were in known buildings, the caseload was published every two weeks, and the spending was reported in near-real time. HomeBASE moved those families into private rentals scattered across the state — and the reporting stopped at almost exactly the same moment.

The population didn't shrink. It dispersed.

Emergency shelter caseload fell from its peak to roughly 4,800 families, and the state is shrinking the system toward ~3,200 units. But 7,700+ families are on HomeBASE, which pays up to $30,000 over two years toward private-market rent. Those households are still being housed at public expense — they are simply no longer housed anywhere the public can count them.

What we could not verify: the November 2025 closeout supplemental budget contained language requiring public biweekly spending reports and regular HomeBASE reporting — including household size, age, gender and immigration status. We could not confirm from public sources whether that provision was enacted, or whether any such HomeBASE report has since been published. If you can point to one, open an issue and this page will be corrected.

The questions this raises

None of this establishes wrongdoing, and the reporting lapse has a mundane explanation available: the statute requiring real-time disclosure was written to expire with fiscal 2025, and it did. But the effect is the same either way — the most expensive housing program the state runs is now also the least visible one. How many HomeBASE households are there today, in which municipalities, at what average cost, for how long, and how many are newly arrived? Every one of those was answerable for emergency shelter. None is answerable for HomeBASE from public records.

The Math

Under Biden: ~10.8M encountered nationwide. ~600K interior removals. = ~7.4M net stayed in the US.
Census Vintage 2025 (Jan 2026): NIM peaked at 2.7M in 2024, then plunged to 1.3M in 2025. Projected ~321K for 2026. US pop growth slowed to 0.5%.
Trump 2.0: FY2025 Southwest Border apprehensions fell to 237,538 — lowest since 1970 — down 84% from 1,530,523 in FY2024 (CBP). USBP recorded zero releases for eight consecutive months as of DHS's January 2026 statement.
FY2026 so far: Oct 2025–Jul 2026 = 80,882 Border Patrol apprehensions at the Southwest border (99,158 nationwide) over the fiscal year's first 10 months. Within that span the monthly Southwest total has risen from 7,988 in Oct 2025 to 9,295 in Jul 2026. CBP has not published the OFO port-of-entry component for FY2026, so no total-encounter figure exists for the year (CBP monthly tables, summed).

Southwest Land Border Encounters by Fiscal Year

Two different measures — read them separately. Total encounters = Border Patrol apprehensions between ports of entry plus Office of Field Operations (OFO) inadmissibles at ports of entry. USBP encounters counts the Border Patrol component only, and is therefore always the smaller of the two. Comparing a USBP bar to a total bar is not like-for-like.
The total series now runs to FY2026. It previously stopped at FY2024, on the understanding that CBP's monthly releases had dropped the OFO component. CBP's own SBO encounters export does carry it: 206,133 OFO inadmissibles in FY2025 and 34,880 so far in FY2026, giving totals of 443,671 and 115,762. The export reproduces the already-plotted FY2023 and FY2024 totals to within OHSS's rounding, so these are the same measure continued rather than a second series spliced on.
Sources: FY2017–FY2024 totals and FY2017–FY2023 USBP — DHS Office of Homeland Security Statistics, KHSM CBP Encounters (ohss.dhs.gov/khsm/cbp-encounters), Southwest Land Border series, rounded by OHSS to the nearest 10; last updated Feb 2025 and currently ending at FY2024. FY2024–FY2026 USBP — CBP monthly tables, summed (FY2025, FY2026). The two sources agree where they overlap: OHSS puts FY2024 USBP at 1,530,520, CBP's own months sum to 1,530,523.
Caveats: FY2020–FY2023 encounters include Title 42 expulsions (Mar 2020–May 2023); a person expelled and re-crossing was counted each time, which inflates those years relative to FY2024 onward. FY2025 = full year (Oct 2024–Sep 2025). FY2026* = Oct 2025–Jul 2026 only, 10 of 12 months, so its bar is a partial year and not comparable to the full years beside it.

FY2025 Final: Southwest Border total apprehensions of 237,538 — lowest since 1970, and 84% below FY2024's 1,530,523 (CBP monthly totals, summed). Zero releases by USBP across May–Nov 2025, the seven months DHS reported at the time.

US Net International Migration — The Historic Nosedive

2.7M → 1.3M → 321K: NIM peaked in 2024, then collapsed. SF Fed (Nov 2025) estimates NIM may be even lower at ~500K for 2025. Both reduced immigration and increased emigration are driving the decline. Without immigration, the US working-age population would have started declining in 2012.

Legal vs Illegal — Net Fiscal Impact

Key finding: Legal immigrants are net positive at all government levels. Illegal immigrants are net negative at state/local level, where costs for education, healthcare, and shelter concentrate.
ITEP (2024): Undocumented immigrants pay $96.7B in total taxes — including $25.7B in payroll taxes to Social Security they will never collect.

Regressive Burden — Who Pays as % of Income

Bottom line: Working poor pay 2.1% of income toward immigration costs. Wealthy households pay 0.13%. The burden is deeply regressive — a 16:1 ratio. The communities bearing the heaviest costs (Chelsea, Lawrence, Lynn) also have the lowest per-capita property tax bases in Massachusetts.
