The budget was signed at $63.4 billion. That's the opening figure.

Massachusetts enacts a budget in July and then raises it all year. Two years ago the state enacted $57.6 billion and spent $63.3 billion. Here is how that gap opens, who closes it, and why nobody covers it.

The signing

One number, no vetoes, and a claim that holds up narrowly

Governor Healey signed the FY2027 budget on 9 July 2026. She struck nothing — no line-item vetoes, no returned amendments. The Legislature's budget became law exactly as written. For comparison, she cut $130 million from the previous year's budget before signing it.

It passed the Senate 39–1 and the House 142–6. The headline claim was no new taxes or fees, and on its own terms that is true: no change to the 5% income tax, the 6.25% sales tax, the 26.5¢ motor fuel excise, or the corporate excise.

What the claim doesn't cover

It says nothing about local property taxes, and it says nothing about how much the state will actually spend. Both are decided later, by other people, in rooms that get less coverage.

The mechanism
Supplemental appropriations

The gap is closed by bills nobody writes about

A supplemental budget is a separate bill, passed during the fiscal year, that adds to appropriations already made. They are routine and frequently justified — caseloads move, snow falls, federal reimbursements shift, collective bargaining settles. Nothing about them is secret.

What's true is simply that they arrive after the story has been filed. The July budget gets the front page and the phrase no new taxes. The $5.7 billion that followed it in FY2025 arrived in pieces, across months, each too small and too technical to cover.

The practical test

When you next read a state budget figure, ask which of two things it is: what was appropriated in July, or what was spent by June. Almost every number in circulation is the first. Almost every number that matters is the second.

What a $63.4 billion budget actually costs you

The tempting arithmetic is $63.4 billion divided by 2.83 million households, which gives $22,410. That number is real but it is not what a household pays, and anyone quoting it that way is wrong. Massachusetts does not raise $63.4 billion from Massachusetts households.

Where it does reach you: the local chain

State income and sales tax rates did not move. What moves is your property tax, and the state budget is one input into it.

01

The state sends aid

$9.02 billion goes to cities and towns — $7.658B of Chapter 70 school aid, restricted to schools, plus $1.363B of unrestricted UGGA.

02

Aid grows slower than costs

UGGA rose 3.0%. Municipal wages, health insurance, energy and special education generally rise faster. The difference is a gap in the town budget.

03

Proposition 2½ caps the fix

A town may raise its total levy by only 2.5% a year, plus new construction. Anything beyond that needs a ballot override.

04

It lands on your bill

The statewide average single-family bill is $8,113, on an average assessed value of $742,986 — an effective rate of 1.092%.

Being fair about this

State aid is one input among several. Towns also control their own headcount, contracts and service levels, and a town whose costs outrun its aid has usually made local choices too. The claim here is narrow and defensible: aid rising 3.0% against faster-rising municipal costs puts upward pressure on the property tax, which is the tax households feel most directly and the one no state budget headline mentions.

And the overrun? A supplemental appropriation does not raise your rate in-year — it is paid from revenue arriving above benchmark, or from reserves. What it does is spend money that could have gone to the stabilisation fund or to tax relief, and it lifts the base that next year's budget grows from. The cost is deferred, not billed.

What's next
What this budget sets up for FY2028

Two things in this budget only bite next year

Chapter 70 has reached the end of its ramp

School aid rises $793.5 million to $7.658 billion, up 11.6% — the largest single increase in the budget. It is also the final year of the Student Opportunity Act's seven-year schedule. That increase is a completion payment, not a new rate of growth. From FY2028 the ramp is gone, and the budget simultaneously reconvenes the Foundation Budget Review Commission to re-examine the formula. Districts that built recurring costs on it are looking at a step down.

Local aid rose 3.0%, and your tax bill is set locally

Unrestricted General Government Aid — the money towns can spend on anything — rose $40 million to $1.363 billion, or 3.0%. That roughly tracks inflation, so it does not close the gap between what municipal services cost and what towns collect. That gap is closed at town meeting, under Proposition 2½.

Which is why record local aid and a higher property tax bill are both true about the same year. The statewide average single-family bill is $8,113.

The line worth watching

The State Retiree Benefits Trust deposit has fallen from $550M in FY2024 to $400M now — down 27% — while the same budget creates a reserve to enhance pension cost-of-living adjustments. Pre-funding falls on the worse-funded promise while the larger one grows. Neither move appears in the “no new taxes or fees” framing, and both land on future budgets rather than this one. Full detail on the pensions & OPEB dashboard.

Accounting restructurings are excluded, because they are not policy changes: the Student Opportunity Act reserve folding into Chapter 70, the C3 childcare move to line 3000-1045, the Free Community College renumbering, and the payroll split at every sheriff’s office. A raw sort would show those as the largest cuts in the budget, and they are not cuts at all.

What to take from this

The FY2027 budget raises no tax rates. That claim is accurate. It is also the smallest of the three things that determine what Massachusetts costs you next year — the other two being what the state ends up spending, and what your town has to raise once state aid falls short.

The $63.4 billion is real. It is just the number at the start.
